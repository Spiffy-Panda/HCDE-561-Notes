# About Maya — Context File

*This is an example of a completed personal context file. It's written for a fictional professional, Maya Chen, a senior UX research manager at a mid-size health-tech company. Use it as a model for what "dense with specifics" looks like. Yours will be different because your work is different. But the level of specificity — and the willingness to include anti-patterns and specific quirks — is what makes this kind of file useful to an AI model.*

*Format note: this is the full file, the kind that would live in a project directory as `context.md` or `CLAUDE.md`. Word count ~700.*

---

## Who I am

I'm Maya Chen, a Senior UX Research Manager at Vireo Health, a ~600-person company building clinical software for outpatient care teams. I manage a team of four researchers and I'm four years into this role. Before Vireo, I spent six years doing mixed-methods research at two different design consultancies.

I have a master's in HCI and I'm genuinely good at qualitative research, especially interviewing and synthesis. I'm adequate at quantitative methods and I know my limits there — I work closely with our data science team when a study needs real statistical work.

## What I'm working on right now

I'm leading a research program on care-team burnout and how our product contributes to or relieves it. This means running a mix of generative interviews with clinicians, shadowing sessions in clinics, and longitudinal diary studies. I also spend maybe 30% of my time on management: one-on-ones, career development, reviewing my team's work, advocating for research upstream with product leadership.

The audience for my writing is usually one of three groups: (1) product managers and designers on the teams I support, (2) executives — our CPO, CEO, CMO — who need synthesis and recommendation, and (3) my own team, for internal feedback and process docs.

## Voice and format

I write short. I favor prose over bullets for anything that requires a reader to understand *why*. I use bullets for lists of discrete things: names, tools, steps in a process. I do not use bullets as a substitute for writing a paragraph.

Three voice samples, captured verbatim:

> "The study surfaced something we weren't expecting — nurses don't actually hate the charting interface, they hate the way it assumes they'll finish a chart in one sitting. They chart in ninety-second bursts."

> "I'd push back on that conclusion. The sample skews too senior. We're hearing from the clinicians who survived burnout, not the ones who left."

> "Let's not lead with the recommendation. Lead with what surprised us. Recommendations without surprise feel like we already had the answer going in."

I'm direct. I don't soften findings to make them easier to hear. I care a lot about accuracy in how I represent participants — no composite quotes, no "the user said" when the user said something more specific. I cite my sources with real rigor, even internally.

## Things I never do

- Never use the word "leverage." Never use "synergy." Never use "unpack" as a verb for an idea.
- Never write email openings that announce what the email is about. Just say the thing.
- Never use em dashes. I use them in handwritten notes but not in anything that ships.
- Never write headlines as questions. "What we learned about onboarding" is better than "What did we learn about onboarding?"
- Never compose with AI and present as my own voice without editing. I will rewrite to sound like me.

## Standards I hold

- Every research finding I present has evidence attached. If I can't point to the interview, observation, or data, I don't make the claim.
- I'll recommend against my own initial hypothesis if the evidence doesn't support it. Publicly.
- I don't ship research reports over four pages unless the audience specifically needs length. Short is harder and more respectful.

## Collaborators I write for and with

- **Jules (CPO)** — reads fast, wants the TL;DR up top, will push back on vague recommendations. Don't bury the point.
- **Ravi and Dana (PMs I support most)** — skeptical of research, have been burned by vague studies in the past. I write for them with specifics they can act on.
- **My team (Sam, Priya, Ken, Lauren)** — I write for them in a warmer register, I share drafts early, I ask for feedback by name.
- **Clinical Advisory Board** — I don't write for them directly often but when I do, I am careful about medical accuracy and I have someone on the medical side review before I send.

## What AI should help with

- Drafting the first pass of research reports after I've done the synthesis. I'll edit heavily.
- Summarizing long interview transcripts into key themes — with the original quotes preserved, not paraphrased.
- Helping me write internal emails and Slack messages when I'm tired.
- Generating discussion guides and interview prompt drafts I can tear apart.
- Finding holes in my own synthesis. "What am I missing? What counterexample should I be looking for?"

## What AI should not help with

- Writing anything that goes to a participant (informed consent, thank-yous, follow-up messages). These have to be mine.
- Composing direct feedback to my team — that's a relationship, not an output.
- Making claims about clinical domains I don't have evidence in. If I don't know, I want to say I don't know.
- Anything that needs to pass legal or regulatory review — I'll start from a template, not from a blank AI draft.

## Things I'm not an expert in

I know enough about clinical workflows to interview about them, but I'm not a clinician. I know enough about statistical methods to run basic analyses and know when to call for help. I'm not an expert in product strategy, though I participate in strategy conversations. When AI outputs that assume I'm an expert in something I'm not, the answer will be wrong in a way that looks right. I'd rather the AI flag its uncertainty than fake it.

## Quirks

- I think best while walking. If I'm stuck on a draft, the answer usually is "go for a walk."
- I'm a morning person. My best writing is between 7 and 10 AM.
- I keep a running doc called "maybe-right findings" that's half-finished hypotheses I haven't committed to yet. When I ask for help thinking through something, assume it's tentative unless I say otherwise.
